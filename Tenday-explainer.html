01

The business problem

A card issuer pays a welcome bonus and a channel cost for every customer it acquires. Most repay it. Some take the bonus and go dormant — and they are invisible in the signup numbers, because a gamer looks exactly like a successful acquisition until months later.

The same question arrives at three moments, with different information available at each:

  • At acquisition — only the channel, the offer and applicant details are known. Can bad outcomes be predicted before any money is spent?
  • In the first 30 days — early behaviour becomes visible. Can eventual value be projected early enough to act?
  • Over the full year — the outcome is clear. Who gamed the offer, what was each group worth, and where should the next rupee go?
Tenday answers all three, and establishes where along that timeline the signal actually lives.
02

What this platform is

Banks give you money to sign up for a credit card. "Spend ₹50,000 in your first three months and we'll give you ₹5,000 cashback." That's a welcome bonus.

The bank is making a bet. It costs them real money — the ₹5,000 bonus, plus maybe ₹1,500 they paid Google or an affiliate to put the ad in front of you. So roughly ₹6,500 out the door before you've spent a rupee. They're betting you'll use the card for years and they'll earn that back through the small fee they collect on every purchase you make.

For most people, that bet works out. For some people, it doesn't.

The problem in one sentence: some people sign up purely for the bonus. They spend fast to hit the target, claim the ₹5,000, and never use the card again. The bank paid ₹6,500 and got almost nothing back. This is called gaming the offer, and the bank can't tell these people apart from real customers until months later.

The platform answers three questions about that:

  1. Who did it, and what did it cost?
  2. Could we have seen it coming — and how early?
  3. So what should the bank change?

That third one is the point. A project that just finds bad customers is a list. A project that says "here's what to do differently" is analysis.

The words that keep coming up

Click any term for a plain explanation. These are the five that matter; the rest is detail.

The pipeline is built on , running on , storing data in , writing the code in , and the reason for all of it is a thing called .

Spark is a tool for doing calculations on data that's too big for one computer. Instead of one machine chewing through 27 million rows, Spark splits the rows across many machines, each does its share, and the results get combined. Think of counting a warehouse of boxes: one person takes a week, twenty people take an afternoon. Spark is the manager who divides up the aisles and adds up the totals.
Databricks is a website that rents you those machines and gives you a notebook to type into — like Jupyter, but the code runs on a cluster instead of a laptop. This ran on the free tier, with no servers to set up; that's the whole product.
Delta Lake is a file format with a safety net. Ordinary data files have no rules: if a job crashes halfway through writing, you're left with half a file and no way to know. Delta keeps a log of every change, so a write either fully happens or doesn't happen at all — and you can go back and look at yesterday's version. It's what makes a folder of files behave like a proper database table.
PySpark is just Python code that talks to Spark. df.groupBy("customer_id").agg(...) looks almost exactly like pandas — the difference is that pandas works in one machine's memory while PySpark ships the work out to a cluster.
Big data just means "won't fit comfortably in one machine's memory". There's no magic number. These 26.7 million transactions were about 4.5 GB — awkward on a laptop, fine on a cluster. And worth being precise about: when measured, pandas actually could have handled it. Spark was chosen because the approach keeps working at ten times the size, not because pandas fell over.

Two more worth knowing:

A and a .

Window function — you already know this one from SQL. It's SUM(amount) OVER (PARTITION BY customer_id ORDER BY date): a running total, calculated separately for each customer, in date order. That single idea is how the platform finds the day each customer crossed their spending target. PySpark spells it slightly differently, but it's the same thing you'd write in Postgres.
Clustering means letting the computer find natural groups without telling it what to look for. You give it numbers about each customer — how much they spend, how spread out it is, how fast they hit the target — and it sorts them into piles where the people in each pile resemble each other. You don't say "find the gamers". You say "find groups", then look at each group and name it.

Step one: building the data

Real bank transaction data doesn't exist publicly — it's some of the most private data there is. So the first step was a program that generates a realistic bank.

This sounds like a shortcut. It isn't, and here's why it's actually better for this question:

  • The size is controlled. The analysis needed enough rows that distributed processing was a genuine choice: 200,000 customers, producing 26.7 million transactions.
  • The right answer is known. The generator decided in advance that 14% of customers would behave like gamers, so every method could be graded against the truth. With real data nobody knows the answer, so nothing can be verified.
The trap avoided. Generating people and then writing a rule that finds exactly those people proves nothing. So the generator deliberately makes the groups overlap and blurry. Early churners lose interest and go quiet, which looks like gaming. Some gamers behave mildly. The models have to do real work to separate them.

The generator makes five tables:

That last table is the answer key, and it stayed in a sealed envelope. It was never fed into a model — only opened at the end, to mark the work. Mixed into the inputs, it would have let the models read the answers off the back of the book.

The data was made dirty on purpose

Real data is never clean, so the generator broke some of it:

Step two: turning 27 million rows into 200,000

There is one row per purchase. But the question is about people, not purchases. So everything is squashed down: 27 million transactions become 200,000 rows, one per customer, with columns describing how that person behaves.

This is just GROUP BY customer_id — the same idea as in any database, running on a cluster.

The familiar SQL

SELECT
  customer_id,
  SUM(amount)  AS total_spend,
  COUNT(*)     AS txn_count,
  MAX(day)     AS last_active_day
FROM transactions
GROUP BY customer_id

The PySpark equivalent

txns.groupBy("customer_id").agg(
    F.sum("amount").alias("total_spend"),
    F.count("*").alias("txn_count"),
    F.max("day").alias("last_active_day"),
)

Same operation. Different spelling. This is most of what "learning PySpark" actually means.

The columns built, and why each one matters

Why days_to_qualify needed a window function. To know when someone crossed ₹50,000 you need a running total of their spending in date order, then the first day it goes over. That's SUM(...) OVER (PARTITION BY customer_id ORDER BY day). It's the one genuinely non-trivial piece of the pipeline, and it produced the single most useful measure in the analysis.

The one clever bit: three separate versions

Here's the idea that makes this more than a standard analysis. The same features are built three times over three different time windows:

Layer 1 uses only what's on the application form — which channel they came from, which offer, their age band, income band, credit limit. Nothing about spending, because on day zero nobody has spent anything.

Layer 2 adds the first 30 days. Layer 3 sees the whole year.

The rule enforced everywhere: a layer may only use information that genuinely existed at that point in time. Every behavioural column is named d30_ or full_ so a mistake is visible at a glance. A full_ column appearing in a Layer 2 model would mean letting the model see the future — and the entire comparison would be meaningless.

Step three: sorting people into groups

That leaves 200,000 rows of numbers. An algorithm called K-Means sorts them into piles of similar people.

K-Means works like this: pick how many piles you want (that's the "k"), scatter that many markers randomly across the data, assign each person to their nearest marker, move each marker to the centre of the people assigned to it, repeat until nothing moves. That's the whole algorithm.

Two practical things had to be right:

Scaling. K-Means measures distance. Total spend runs into the hundreds of thousands; a share is between 0 and 1. Without rescaling everything onto a comparable footing, spend would drown out everything else and the groups would just be "spent a lot" versus "spent a little". The money columns are also log-transformed because a few enormous spenders would otherwise distort every boundary.

Choosing k. There's a standard score called silhouette that says how tidy the groups are. It preferred k=4. We used k=6 anyway.

This is the most important judgement call in the analysis. At low k, the algorithm lumped gamers together with early churners — because both go quiet and both spend early. Mathematically tidy, operationally useless: you can't act on a group that's half innocent people. At k=6 they separate, because days_to_qualify pulls them apart (10 days versus 96).

The line to say: "I chose k on business separability rather than silhouette, because the silhouette-optimal k merged the segment I was trying to isolate with its nearest look-alike."

Step four: the model that didn't work

The obvious way to catch bad behaviour is anomaly detection — find the weird ones. We tested a method called Isolation Forest, which hunts for data points that are easy to separate from everyone else.

It did badly. Out of everyone it flagged, only 43% were actually gamers. The behavioural grouping got 99.8%.

Why it lost, and this is the interesting part. Anomaly detection assumes bad things are rare and strange. But gaming isn't rare — it's 14% of the book — and gamers all behave in the same way as each other. They're not oddballs. They're a crowd.

Meanwhile the genuinely odd customers are the best ones: people spending ₹406,000 a year really are statistical outliers. So the model kept flagging the bank's most profitable customers as suspicious.

The same mistake surfaced twice. A first attempt to find the gaming group automatically picked whichever group had the highest average anomaly score — and it chose Minimal Engagers: people with ₹8,751 of spend across 12 transactions. The most "isolated" people in the data, because they barely did anything at all. Not gamers. Just quiet.

It was fixed by describing gaming in terms of behaviour instead — qualifies fast, redeems fast, buys near-cash, goes quiet — and ranking the groups on that. It picked the right one immediately.

Report the failure. Most portfolio projects only show what worked, which reads as either luck or selective editing. Yours has a real methodological finding: the wrong tool failed for a reason I can articulate. That's worth more than a clean score.

Step five: the main findings

This is the heart of the analysis: at what point does the bank have enough information to tell?

The same kind of model, on the same people, with the same targets — changing only what the model was allowed to see. Click between the two:

What the model can see

Finding one — the application form tells us almost nothing

Channel, campaign, age band, income band, region and credit limit together predict gaming at 0.62 on a scale where 0.5 is a coin flip. They explain 9% of how much a customer will eventually be worth. Ninety-one percent is invisible on signup day.

So screening applicants harder would reject good people at nearly the same rate as bad ones. This is a real result, not a failed model. Measuring how little can be known is the finding.

Finding two — one month of behaviour changes everything

Add the first 30 days and the ability to predict a customer's value jumps from 9% to 66%. Gamers become almost completely separable. And 30 days is early — most of the bonus money hasn't gone out the door yet, so there's still time to act.

The recommendation: stop trying to filter at the front door. Watch the first month instead. Change the offer so the bonus needs spending sustained over three months rather than a lump sum, or hold the payout until day 90, or don't count gift cards toward the target.

Be honest about one number. The Layer 2 gaming score came out at a perfect 1.000. That's too good, and it is worth stating plainly. By day 30, 77% of gamers have hit the target and almost nobody else has, so one column nearly gives the game away. It's not leakage — that's genuinely visible on day 30 — but real bank data would be messier. The defensible reading is "the signal is unambiguous by day 30 in this data", not "the model is perfect".

Finding three — the offer is the lever, not the channel

We assumed some acquisition channels were bringing in worse customers than others. They are not, or not by much. The campaign is doing almost all of the work.

A larger bonus does not buy a better customer. It buys someone who will do precisely enough to qualify and then stop.

What this changes. Before reallocating budget between channels, restructure the offers themselves. That is where the ₹5,000 sits.

Step six: turning it into a decision

Findings are nice. A decision is better. Two numbers are combined for every customer — how much they're predicted to be worth, and how likely they are to be gaming — and everyone is sorted into five buckets:

TierWhat to doPeopleActually worth
1Acquire more like this36,178+₹2,117
2Acquire, but watch them early32,905+₹7,936
3Acquire only on cheap offers49,116−₹335
4Watch list15,741−₹161
5Don't target66,060−₹2,950

Look at tier 2. It has the highest actual value of any tier — ₹7,936 — and an elevated gaming rate. That's not a bug. High value and high risk overlap because both involve spending heavily. A bank that simply rejected everyone who looked risky would be throwing out its most profitable customers. Which is exactly why tier 2's instruction is "monitor", not "reject".

One technical point worth knowing

Ranking customers by predicted value needs a prediction for everyone — but the model was trained on most of them, and a model always looks smarter on data it has already seen. So we used out-of-fold prediction: split everyone into five parts, and predict each part using a model trained only on the other four. Every customer gets scored by a model that never saw them. The honest score came out at 0.605, very close to the 0.658 you got on properly held-out data — which tells you the model generalises.

The caveat you must state. Dropping tier 5 lifts the modelled profit from ₹12.4 crore to ₹31.9 crore. That looks spectacular, and you must not oversell it. Tier 5 is a third of the entire customer base and only 26% of it is gamers — so most of that gain is just from not paying bonuses to low-value customers in general, not from catching gaming. No bank declines a third of applicants. It sizes the opportunity; it doesn't promise it.

Step seven: letting an AI write the summary

The last piece takes all your results and has a language model write the memo a manager would actually read.

The important design choice: the AI never touches the data. It doesn't see a single transaction and it does no arithmetic. Every number is calculated by your Spark and scikit-learn code first, packed into a small JSON file of 86 facts, and handed over. The model's only job is turning facts into sentences.

Then you check its work. Every number in the text it produces is pulled out and matched against those 86 facts. Anything that doesn't match gets flagged.

It caught a real one. An early run wrote "High-Value Core contains 8451". The true figure is 84,517 — the model dropped a digit. It reads perfectly fluently and you'd never spot it by eye in a page of prose. The check caught it instantly.

That's the difference between "I called an API" and "I deployed a language model with a verification layer". Only one of those is worth talking about.

And you should also say what it can't do. The check confirms the numbers are real. It can't tell whether they're the right numbers to highlight — an early version rambled about marketing channels when the analysis showed channels barely matter. Accuracy and judgement are different problems, and only one of them can be automated.

12

What we recommend

Four changes, all of which act inside the window where the behaviour is actually visible. None of them requires declining anybody.

Restructure the offers
Replace lump-sum spending targets with sustained spend across three months. A target that cannot be cleared in ten days cannot be gamed in ten days.
Exclude near-cash categories from qualifying spend
Gift cards, money transfers and wholesale clubs are the mechanism. Removing them removes most of the route.
Hold the bonus payout to day 90
Paying at qualification means paying before we know anything. A short delay moves the payout to the point where behaviour is visible.
Monitor month one rather than screening at the door
Track early-tenure behaviour on new accounts instead of tightening approval criteria that we now know carry almost no signal.
Do not simply reject everyone who looks risky. The second tier is the most valuable group in the portfolio and carries elevated risk signals — because heavy spending is what makes a customer valuable, and heavy early spending is what makes them resemble a bonus-seeker. A blunt screen would decline our best customers alongside the worst.
13

What this cannot tell you

Three limits, stated plainly, because the analysis is worth acting on only if its boundaries are understood.

The portfolio is simulated

Real card transaction data cannot be shared, so this book was generated to mirror acquisition economics. Customer types were built with known behavioural patterns, so the separation here is cleaner than production would be. Treat these results as an upper bound on what is achievable, not an estimate of it.

The ₹19.5 crore is a bound, not a forecast

Removing the bottom group entirely would lift modelled value from ₹12.4 crore to ₹31.9 crore. But that group is a third of all customers and only a quarter of it is bonus-seekers, so most of the gain comes from not paying bonuses to low-value customers generally. No issuer declines a third of applicants. It sizes the opportunity; it does not promise it.

Changing the offer will change behaviour

These figures describe customers acquired under the current terms. Restructuring the offer changes who applies and how they act, so the effect of the recommendations cannot be read directly off this analysis. A ninety-day trial on a single campaign would settle it.

The whole thing, in order: build a realistic bank → clean 27 million messy rows → squash them into 200,000 customer profiles across three time windows → group people by behaviour → catch the gamers → measure how early they could have been caught → turn it into a targeting policy → have an AI write the memo and check its numbers.

In one line. We cannot tell who will game an offer from their application. We can tell almost perfectly from their first month. So the fix is the offer and the first thirty days — not the front door.
